# From Elections to Ongoing Citizen Participation

### A personal proposal for citizen proposals, expert committees, and public voting

This is an idea I have been thinking about and want to put into the public domain. I am sharing it because I believe citizens should have a more direct and continuous way to take part in public decisions, not only a chance to choose representatives at election time.

I do not present this as a finished system or as a claim that nobody has explored digital democracy before. It is a proposal that I hope researchers, universities, civic organizations, developers, and public institutions will examine, criticize, and develop if they find something useful in it.

## 1. The question behind the idea

In representative political systems, citizens usually choose people to represent them. But after an election, citizens may have little direct influence over particular laws, public projects, or important policy decisions until the next election.

I began asking whether technology could make participation more continuous. Could a citizen put forward a proposal, have it examined by people who understand its subject, and then let the wider public decide whether it should move forward?

My interest in this question was partly influenced by Bitcoin and the work published under the name Satoshi Nakamoto. What interested me was the possibility of using shared rules and verification to reduce dependence on a single central party. I understand that political decisions are very different from financial transactions, so I do not think Bitcoin's design can simply be copied into government. It was an inspiration for asking a broader question about trust, verification, and collective decision-making.

## 2. The basic idea

The idea is an online system where eligible citizens can submit proposals about public matters. A proposal could concern a law, a government decision, a public project, the budget, or an economic policy.

The proposal would not go straight to a country-wide vote. It would first be sent to a committee that deals with that subject. People who want to serve on a committee would have to provide verifiable evidence of relevant education, professional qualifications, or experience.

The committee would examine the proposal, discuss its likely effects, and vote on it. If it receives the required majority in the committee (for example, half plus one), it moves to a vote among eligible citizens. The public vote would determine whether citizens support the proposal.

The central point is the order of these steps: a proposal begins with a citizen, receives a review from people with relevant knowledge, and then goes to the public for a wider decision. Expertise is part of the process, but the final public choice is not handed over to experts.

## 3. How I imagine it working

1. **Verified participation.** A person proves that they are eligible to participate. My initial thought was to begin with Iraqi citizens aged 18 and over, but the model could be adapted to other countries or communities.
2. **Submitting a proposal.** Any eligible citizen can put forward an idea or proposed change.
3. **Referral to the relevant committee.** The proposal is directed to the committee responsible for that subject, such as finance, education, health, or law.
4. **Review and committee vote.** Committee members examine the proposal and discuss its possible consequences. They then vote. A proposal that receives the required majority proceeds to the public vote.
5. **Public vote.** Eligible citizens vote on the proposal. The result should be recorded and made available for checking.
6. **Following the result.** Where the relevant institution has the authority and legal ability to act on the result, the next steps and the status of implementation should be made clear to the public.

For example, a citizen might propose a change to currency policy, such as removing zeros from the currency, or suggest a public financial measure. The proposal would first be examined by the relevant financial and economic committee. If it passed that stage, citizens would then have the opportunity to vote on it.

## 4. Why include both experts and citizens?

A public decision can have technical consequences that are difficult to assess without relevant knowledge. At the same time, technical knowledge alone cannot decide what a society values or what trade-offs its citizens are willing to accept.

That is why I see two different roles in this model. The committee examines the proposal from the point of view of its subject and helps make the consequences understandable. The citizens then make the wider choice through voting.

This arrangement would need clear rules. Committee qualifications should be verifiable, and the reasons behind a committee decision should be open to examination. Otherwise, a committee could become a new closed political authority instead of serving the public.

## 5. The role of technology

I originally thought about blockchain because identity verification, transparent records, and confidence in the counting process are important to this kind of system. But I am not saying that every part must use blockchain, or that blockchain by itself makes an election secure.

A real system would have to answer difficult questions: how to confirm that a participant is eligible without exposing private information; how to prevent duplicate or fraudulent voting; how to protect the secrecy of each person's vote; and how independent observers could verify the result.

Those are technical and legal problems that need qualified research and testing. I have not built or security-tested such a system, and this document should not be treated as proof that those problems have already been solved.

## 6. Limits that must be taken seriously

A majority vote cannot be allowed to remove fundamental rights or protections. Any real implementation would need to respect the constitution and laws of the country or institution using it, protect minorities, and provide a way to challenge procedural errors.

Also, an online vote does not automatically become a legally binding government decision. That would depend on the legal authority given to the system and on the institutions responsible for acting on the result. In a first application, the system might only provide recommendations or support decisions within a voluntary organization. A national application would require a much stronger legal and security basis.

## 7. What this proposal is — and is not

I know that digital participation and online democracy already exist in different forms. Projects such as [Decidim](https://decidim.org/) are important examples, and they should be studied rather than ignored.

I am not claiming to have invented online voting, participatory democracy, blockchain governance, or expert consultation. The question I want others to examine is whether the particular sequence described here — a citizen proposal, review and vote by a qualified subject committee, followed by a vote of the wider public — can be designed in a fair, secure, and useful way.

This is an initial concept, not a complete technical specification, a legal proposal, or a working product. It needs comparison with existing projects and research, and it may need substantial changes. I would rather have its weaknesses identified honestly than present it as a finished solution.

## 8. Why I am making it public

I do not want this idea to depend on me, or to become something that only I can develop or authorize. I do not have the resources to build and operate a system of this scale. I am publishing the proposal so that a university, research center, civic organization, public institution, or independent team can take it further if they believe it is worth exploring.

Anyone is free to study it, criticize it, change it, build on it, or decide not to use it. I am not asking an institution to accept the proposal as it stands. I am asking whether the idea has enough value to deserve serious review, and what would have to change before it could be tested in practice.
